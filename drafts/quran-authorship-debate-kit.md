# Who Wrote the Qur'an? — Debate and Rehearsal Kit

Status: private working draft based on the current SecondFaith knowledge base. This is debate preparation, not a source-verified publication. Source locators below are leads already recorded in the project; recheck exact wording and context before a formal or public debate.

## 1. The position to defend

Do not take on the unnecessarily difficult claim that one person sat down and wrote every word of the Qur'an exactly as a modern author writes a book.

Defend this instead:

> The Qur'an we possess is adequately explained as the result of human proclamation, composition, transmission, collection, selection, and standardization in a particular historical environment. The claim that its ultimate author is Allah is an additional theological conclusion that requires independent evidence.

An even shorter version:

> I am not claiming to know every hand through which the Qur'an passed. I am arguing that the evidence shows a human historical process, while divine authorship has not been demonstrated.

This position does **not** require you to prove:

- that God does not exist;
- that Muhammad consciously fabricated everything;
- that every verse was altered;
- that all traditional reports are historically reliable;
- or that the Qur'an lacks beauty, influence, or moral value.

It requires you to show two things:

1. The observable evidence is expected under an ordinary human-origin model.
2. The proposed evidence for Allah's authorship does not distinguish that hypothesis from a human one.

## 2. Define the question before debating it

The sentence “Allah wrote the Qur'an” can mean several different things. Ask your discussion partner which one they mean:

- Allah is the ultimate source of every Arabic word.
- Allah supplied ideas while Muhammad expressed them in his own language.
- Gabriel delivered exact words which Muhammad recited exactly.
- Muhammad recited exact words, but later humans collected and standardized them.
- The present Hafs text is letter-for-letter identical to a heavenly original.

These are not the same claim. Do not allow movement between them during the debate. If the claim changes, politely say: “That is a different version of divine authorship; let us identify which version we are evaluating.”

Also distinguish:

- **Origin:** where the words or ideas first came from.
- **Proclamation:** who first delivered them publicly.
- **Transmission:** how people remembered and copied them.
- **Canonization:** who decided which written form became authoritative.

The historical evidence directly shows human beings at the last three stages. The dispute concerns whether a divine source must be added at the first stage.

## 3. The governing argument

Use this logical structure throughout:

1. Human authorship and transmission predict a text expressed in the language, concepts, controversies, social arrangements, and stories of its environment.
2. They predict responses to the leader's immediate political, domestic, and rhetorical circumstances.
3. They predict dependence on memory, written fragments, named transmitters, editorial procedures, disagreement, and official standardization.
4. These are the kinds of features found in the Qur'an and in the Islamic tradition's account of its formation.
5. None of these features requires a supernatural cause.
6. Therefore, a human historical explanation is sufficient unless independent evidence establishes divine authorship.

The conclusion is comparative, not absolute:

> The available observations do not force us from “humans produced and preserved this text” to “Allah authored its words.”

Do not say that a human process logically excludes divine involvement. A believer can always say Allah worked through the process. Your sharper reply is:

> That preserves divine authorship as a possibility, but it does not supply evidence for it. What observation distinguishes “Allah worked through these events” from the same events occurring without Allah?

## 4. Three-minute opening statement

The question tonight is not whether the Qur'an calls itself revelation. It plainly does. The question is whether we have sufficient reason to conclude that Allah, rather than human beings, is the source of its words.

We should begin with what both sides can observe. The Qur'an appeared through a human speaker in seventh-century Arabia. It uses human language. It addresses local disputes, responds to named opponents, regulates the speaker's household and community, and draws on stories and religious concepts already present in the wider late-antique environment. After Muhammad's death, according to the Islamic tradition itself, human beings gathered material from memories and written pieces, selected a standard form, copied it, distributed it, and destroyed competing written material. Whatever theological interpretation we add, that is a human historical process.

My argument is not that I can identify the private intention behind every verse. Nor must I prove that Muhammad alone wrote a modern-style book. My position is that ordinary human causes explain the evidence we actually possess. Human beings can produce eloquent and influential texts. They can sincerely believe they are inspired. They can adapt inherited stories, respond to circumstances, transmit material orally, disagree over recitation, and later standardize a canon. We know all of these processes occur.

The alternative claim is much larger: that an invisible, perfect and omniscient being selected every word and communicated it without independently observable access to the event. That claim cannot be established merely because the text says it is true. A book's testimony about itself is part of the claim, not independent confirmation of the claim. Nor does literary uniqueness establish divinity: every important text is unique in some respects, and no objective rule connects failure to imitate a style with authorship by God.

The evidence I will present is cumulative. First, the traditional collection accounts show posthumous human assembly and official standardization. Second, parts of the text track immediate human needs and circumstances in ways naturally expected from a human voice. Third, the principal arguments offered for divine authorship—self-testimony, preservation and inimitability—do not logically identify Allah as the source. Divine authorship remains a faith interpretation that can be placed over the history, but the history itself does not demonstrate it.

## 5. Core argument one: the received text has a human formation history

### The argument

1. On the Islamic tradition's own account, the Qur'an was not left by Muhammad as a single, personally authorized bound codex.
2. After deaths among reciters at Yamama created fear that material might be lost, Abu Bakr commissioned Zayd ibn Thabit to collect Qur'anic material from written pieces and human memories.
3. Later disagreement in recitation led Uthman to commission standard copies, instruct a committee how to decide linguistic disagreement, distribute the resulting text, and order other Qur'anic written materials burned.
4. Reports also preserve memories of wording considered revealed but absent from the codex.
5. These are acts of human preservation, judgment, selection, and political standardization.
6. Consequently, the authority of the received codex cannot be established merely by asserting that the original revelation was perfect. A separate bridge is required from the alleged revelation to this particular humanly transmitted text.

### Best internal evidence currently available

Treat these as reports within the tradition; the debate point concerns what the tradition itself says.

- **Sahih al-Bukhari 4986:** Zayd's collection under Abu Bakr after concern about reciters killed at Yamama. The project records the material as being gathered from palm stalks, stones or parchments, and memories.
- **Sahih al-Bukhari 4987:** divergent recitation, Uthman's copying project, an instruction to use the Quraysh dialect in case of disagreement, provincial distribution, and the burning of other Qur'anic materials.
- **Sahih al-Bukhari 6830 and Sahih Muslim 1691a:** Umar reportedly says the stoning verse was revealed, recited, understood, and applied, although it is absent from the codex.
- **Sahih Muslim 1452a:** Aisha reportedly speaks of verses concerning ten and then five sucklings, with the relevant recited wording no longer in the codex.

The first two are enough for the central procedural point. The latter two are useful when discussing the claim that every revealed recitation must be present in the codex, but expect the answer of *abrogation of recitation*.

### What this establishes—and what it does not

It establishes that the text's preservation and canonization involved contingent human events and decisions.

It does not by itself establish that Muhammad invented the recitations, or that the standard consonantal text substantially changed what he proclaimed. Say this before your opponent says it.

### Expected response: “Compilation is not authorship”

Reply:

> Correct. I am not confusing them. I am showing that the text available for inspection reaches us through a human process. You still need evidence both that the initial words came from Allah and that this particular canon reliably preserves those words. The collection story cannot itself prove either premise.

### Expected response: “Uthman only unified dialects”

Reply:

> Even if that is the correct theological interpretation, Bukhari's report still describes disagreement, a committee rule for resolving it, official copies, and destruction of other materials. Calling the process preservation does not make it non-human or infallible. What independent method shows that every committee decision reproduces a heavenly original?

Do not claim that all destroyed codices contained radically different Qur'ans. The current project explicitly says that this stronger claim is not established.

### Expected response: “Allah promised to preserve it”

Reply:

> That uses a statement inside the disputed text to guarantee the transmission of the disputed text. It may express Muslim belief, but it is not independent historical evidence that the process was divinely protected.

### Cross-examination for this section

1. Did Muhammad leave a single bound codex that he personally authenticated?
2. Do you accept the basic events reported in Bukhari 4986 and 4987?
3. If there was no risk of loss, why does the report make fear of loss the reason for collection?
4. If there was no meaningful disagreement, why was a decision rule needed and why were other materials destroyed?
5. What evidence—independent of the Qur'an's own assertion—shows that the committee's product corresponds exactly to words from Allah?
6. Do the reports of the stoning and suckling recitations describe revelation absent from today's codex? If yes, in what precise sense is every revealed word preserved?

## 6. Core argument two: the text tracks immediate human circumstances

### The argument

1. A human religious and political leader would be expected to produce pronouncements responding to disputes, alliances, warfare, followers, opponents, marriages, and household tensions.
2. The Qur'an repeatedly contains material closely fitted to such events, including rules and exceptions specific to Muhammad.
3. A divine author could choose to address these events, so this pattern is not a strict disproof of revelation.
4. But the pattern is predicted directly by human authorship, while the divine hypothesis must add the explanation that an omniscient universal author chose to speak in exactly this highly local and personally useful manner.
5. The fit therefore supports the human explanation unless independent evidence favors the divine one.

### Strong examples to rehearse

Use the Qur'anic passages themselves and avoid exaggerated motives.

- **Qur'an 33:37:** the text intervenes in the Zayd–Zaynab episode and legitimates Muhammad's marriage to the former wife of his adopted son, while reforming the legal meaning of adoption.
- **Qur'an 33:50–51:** Muhammad receives marital permissions and discretion framed as privileges specific to him.
- **Qur'an 33:53:** revelation regulates how visitors behave in Muhammad's home and prohibits believers from marrying his widows after him.
- **Qur'an 66:1–5:** revelation intervenes in a domestic conflict involving Muhammad's wives and warns them while supporting him.
- **Sahih al-Bukhari 4788:** in the reported context of Q 33:51, Aisha remarks that Muhammad's Lord hastens to fulfill his desire. Use it as an observation preserved by the tradition, not as a conclusive verdict.

The force is the pattern, not any isolated verse:

> Again and again, the divine voice resolves the immediate affairs of the person through whom that voice is heard—sometimes granting him exceptions, settling domestic problems, protecting his household, or answering personal antagonists. Human authorship explains why the supposedly universal speaker is so consistently aligned with the local speaker's needs.

### Expected response: “Of course revelation addressed real events”

Reply:

> Certainly it could. The question is evidential: what feature tells us that this is God responding to Muhammad's circumstances rather than Muhammad responding to his own circumstances? If both hypotheses predict the same local fit, the local fit cannot be offered as evidence that selects the divine hypothesis.

### Expected response: “The Zaynab marriage abolished a harmful taboo”

Reply:

> That may be the theological or legal purpose. It remains striking that a supposedly universal reform was enacted through a revelation authorizing the messenger's own contested marriage. A purpose can be assigned after the fact; what independent evidence identifies Allah, rather than the interested human participant, as the speaker?

### Expected response: “The Qur'an sometimes criticizes Muhammad, so he cannot be the author”

Reply:

> Self-criticism does not establish non-human authorship. Human authors routinely rebuke themselves, dramatize correction, preserve authority by showing accountability, or sincerely interpret events as divine correction. You would need to show why a particular criticism was impossible or seriously costly for a human claimant to include.

### Cross-examination for this section

1. Can a human author write rules that inconvenience or criticize himself?
2. If yes, why does Qur'anic criticism prove Allah's authorship?
3. What test distinguishes “Allah conveniently granted Muhammad this exception” from “Muhammad presented the exception as revelation”?
4. Why does a timeless final revelation need to regulate the duration of visits inside one man's home?
5. Would you regard similarly convenient revelations in a later religious claimant as evidence for or against that claimant?

## 7. Core argument three: the case for Allah does not meet its burden

The other side is making a positive claim about an unobserved supernatural author. Ask for the evidence and examine each proposed route separately.

### A. The Qur'an says it is from Allah

Reply:

> That establishes what the text claims. It does not independently establish that the claim is true. Otherwise every text claiming divine origin would authenticate itself.

Useful question:

> What method lets us accept the Qur'an's self-testimony but reject the same kind of self-testimony from another religion?

### B. The literary challenge proves divinity

The response has several layers:

1. What exactly counts as “like it”—same rhyme, length, grammar, themes, emotional effect, or historical influence?
2. Who is the neutral judge?
3. What would a successful imitation look like, and would a believer ever accept one?
4. Even if no one equaled it, why would unique literary excellence entail authorship by Allah rather than extraordinary human ability?

The logical point is decisive:

> “No one has produced an agreed equal” does not entail “therefore God wrote it.” A missing human rival is not positive identification of a divine author.

Do not get trapped into composing a sura. The claimant must first provide an objective test and demonstrate that passing or failing it identifies the source.

### C. Muhammad was unlettered

Reply:

> Being unable or unlikely to write a manuscript does not prevent oral composition, dictation, collaboration, memory, exposure to stories, or sincere religious experience. More importantly, inability to read does not imply that Allah is the source; it only rules out one narrow method of human production if the premise is granted.

Ask whether “ummi” definitely means unable to read and write, or a prophet from a people without prior scripture. If the definition becomes disputed, do not let the whole debate depend on it.

### D. Perfect preservation proves divine origin

Reply:

> Preservation and authorship are separate questions. Human beings preserve texts, and a perfectly preserved human statement remains human. Conversely, the traditional reports themselves describe collection, disagreement and standardization, so perfect preservation cannot simply be assumed.

### E. Scientific or prophetic miracles prove it

Ask for one claim at a time and require:

1. the verse's wording in context;
2. the interpretation documented before the alleged discovery or event;
3. a precise, risky prediction rather than flexible imagery;
4. exclusion of ordinary knowledge, coincidence, ambiguity, and reinterpretation;
5. and a logical bridge from an impressive statement to Allah specifically.

Do not chase ten miracle claims rapidly. Make your partner choose the single strongest one.

### F. The Qur'an transformed Arabia

Reply:

> Influence can establish historical power, not supernatural authorship. Human political, philosophical and religious texts have also transformed civilizations. We need a criterion that identifies divine causation rather than importance.

## 8. Supporting argument: the Qur'an belongs to its religious environment

Use this as cumulative context, not as your main proof until the individual sources are checked.

The current knowledge base contains leads involving:

- non-Arabic vocabulary discussed in the Islamic scholarly tradition, including al-Suyuti;
- the pre-Islamic Christian story of the Sleepers of Ephesus and the Qur'anic Companions of the Cave;
- Greek and Syriac Alexander traditions and the Dhu al-Qarnayn narrative;
- pre-Islamic Jewish or rabbinic narrative motifs, including angels bowing to Adam;
- Syriac Christian, Jewish, Arabian, and other late-antique concepts circulating in or around Arabia.

State the inference carefully:

> Resemblance alone does not prove direct copying, and shared vocabulary alone does not prove dependence. What the parallels establish first is availability and cultural continuity. A text drawing upon its environment is expected under human production. Calling the same continuity “divine confirmation” is possible, but that interpretation needs independent support.

### Expected response: “The Qur'an confirms earlier revelation”

Reply:

> That is one theological interpretation of the similarity. Cultural transmission is another. What evidence, independent of the Qur'an's self-description, distinguishes divine confirmation from human reuse of circulating material?

### Do not yet lean heavily on

- precise Syriac re-readings associated with highly revisionist scholarship;
- a claim of direct dependence whenever only a broad motif is shared;
- detailed claims about the dating and exact wording of Alexander manuscripts;
- or a claim that Mecca's international commercial role automatically proves access to every story.

These may become useful after verification, but they are currently easy targets.

## 9. Reserve argument: possible anachronisms

This is useful if your partner bases divine authorship on perfect historical knowledge, but it should not carry your opening case.

### Mary, Aaron and Imran

The Qur'an calls Mary the mother of Jesus “sister of Aaron” at Q 19:27–28 and calls her daughter of Imran at Q 66:12. The earlier biblical genealogy associates Miriam, Moses and Aaron with Amram/Imran.

The critical reading is that the Qur'an conflates Mary the mother of Jesus with Miriam, sister of Moses and Aaron. The standard Muslim replies include:

- “sister of Aaron” is an honorific or lineage designation;
- people were named after earlier prophets and pious persons;
- or “sister” need not mean a literal sibling.

Your reply should be modest:

> Those replies may make the wording logically possible, but the surface combination of “sister of Aaron” and “daughter of Imran” is exactly what a conflation would look like. What positive textual evidence selects the honorific explanation rather than that explanation being introduced to avoid the difficulty?

### Al-Samiri and Haman

The project contains claims about al-Samiri in Moses' era and Haman in Pharaoh's court, but their present evidence is low or contested. Do not use them until the underlying chronology, language, and best Muslim replies have been checked carefully.

## 10. The first-revelation authentication problem

Use this as an epistemic argument, not as a diagnosis of Muhammad.

On the traditional first-revelation narrative, Muhammad experiences a frightening encounter and Khadija brings him to Waraqa ibn Nawfal, who interprets it as the same heavenly messenger associated with Moses. The identification of the event as divine is therefore mediated through human interpretation inside the story.

The point is:

> Even if the experience is granted, an experience and its interpretation are different things. How can an outsider independently determine that its cause was Gabriel sent by Allah rather than a sincere but human religious experience?

Avoid claims about mental illness. They are unnecessary, hard to establish historically, and usually derail the discussion into insult rather than evidence.

## 11. Ten high-value cross-examination questions

Ask one, obtain a clear answer, and follow it. Do not fire all ten at once.

1. What is your single strongest piece of evidence that Allah authored the Qur'an?
2. Is that evidence independent of the Qur'an saying that it is from Allah?
3. What possible observation would count against your conclusion?
4. What feature distinguishes divine revelation from sincere human religious composition?
5. Does literary excellence logically identify an author as God? If so, what is the missing premise?
6. What are the objective criteria, procedure, and neutral judge for the inimitability challenge?
7. Do you agree that the received codex was collected and standardized by people after Muhammad's death?
8. How do you independently establish that their product corresponds exactly to a heavenly original?
9. If a later prophet produced convenient revelations and said God had granted them, would that convenience count as evidence for his claim?
10. Are you arguing that divine authorship is possible, or that the evidence makes it more likely than the human explanation?

Question 10 is especially useful. Much apologetic argument establishes only compatibility or possibility and then speaks as if it established probability or fact.

## 12. Short response cards

### “You cannot prove Muhammad wrote it.”

> I do not need to prove a modern single-author model. The options are not “Muhammad wrote every mark” or “Allah did.” A text can emerge through oral composition, followers, scribes, memories, variants and later canonization. My claim is that the observable process is human and the extra divine attribution has not been demonstrated.

### “You are assuming naturalism.”

> I am not saying miracles are impossible. I am asking what evidence requires one here. We should not add a supernatural cause when the observations are already accounted for by known human processes unless there is evidence that distinguishes the supernatural explanation.

### “Millions memorized it.”

> That may describe later preservation. The relevant questions are how early the mass transmission can be demonstrated, what exactly was memorized, and how that proves the initial source was Allah. Many memorizers would still not turn human words into divine words.

### “There are different readings, not different Qur'ans.”

> Labels do not settle the issue. We can examine whether the differences affect pronunciation, morphology or wording and then ask what level of identity is being claimed. In any case, authorized variation is a theological explanation of textual plurality; it is not independent evidence for a heavenly source.

### “The reports of missing recitations are abrogation.”

> That is an internal doctrine designed to reconcile revelation with absence from the codex. What independent evidence shows that the absent wording was divinely withdrawn rather than lost or excluded through the transmission process?

### “Why would Muhammad include embarrassing criticism?”

> Human writers and leaders can include self-correction, especially when it strengthens an image of accountability or records sincere belief. Criticism is compatible with both hypotheses, so it cannot by itself decide between them.

### “The Qur'an contains knowledge Muhammad could not have known.”

> Choose the strongest single example. Let us establish the wording, the earliest interpretation, the state of contemporary knowledge, the precision of the claim, and whether Allah is the only explanation.

### “No human could write anything like it.”

> Please define the measurable standard and explain why literary unmatchedness entails divine authorship. Without those two steps, the claim is an aesthetic judgment followed by a non sequitur.

### “The Qur'an is too coherent to be human.”

> Humans produce coherent works, and editors can increase coherence. First define and demonstrate the claimed degree of coherence; then show why it exceeds human ability and why Allah specifically follows.

### “You just want to reject Islam.”

> My motive does not decide who authored the text. Let us apply the same evidential standards to this claim that we would apply to any other claimed revelation.

## 13. Traps to avoid

- Do not say “the Qur'an was definitely corrupted” when your evidence establishes collection or variation.
- Do not say “borrowed” when the evidence establishes only a parallel or common environment.
- Do not treat every hadith as simultaneously reliable when useful and unreliable when inconvenient. Say whether you use it as historical evidence or as an internal feature of the tradition's own account.
- Do not argue that personally useful revelation logically disproves God. Argue that it fits the human hypothesis and needs independent authentication.
- Do not rely on dozens of small alleged errors. Three well-understood arguments are stronger than a catalogue you cannot defend.
- Do not mock Arabic, Muhammad, Muslims, or religious experience. It weakens both the conversation and the argument.
- Do not accept the false dichotomy “Muhammad was a liar or Allah wrote it.” Sincere religious interpretation, gradual oral formation, multiple contributors, and later canonization are human possibilities.
- Do not let “possible” become “proved.” Divine accommodation, abrogation, miraculous preservation and confirmation of earlier stories may be possible explanations. Ask what evidence makes them preferable.
- Do not let the debate switch unnoticed from origin to preservation, from preservation to beauty, or from beauty to moral influence.

## 14. A disciplined conversation strategy

### Phase 1: Agreement

Agree on the uncontroversial human surface:

- a human speaker delivered the recitations;
- human listeners remembered them;
- human scribes wrote materials;
- human authorities collected and standardized a codex;
- and human readers transmitted it afterward.

Then identify the exact extra claim: Allah selected and communicated the wording.

### Phase 2: Ask for the best evidence

Do not immediately unload your whole case. Ask:

> What is the strongest reason—not ten reasons, just the strongest one—that you believe Allah authored the words?

Test that argument carefully. If it fails, do not allow it to be replaced by five undeveloped claims without acknowledging the result.

### Phase 3: Present the cumulative human case

Use this order:

1. known human process of collection and standardization;
2. close fit between content and immediate human circumstances;
3. continuity with the surrounding religious environment;
4. lack of an independent discriminator for Allah.

### Phase 4: State the limited conclusion

> I am not claiming that I have disproved every conceivable divine role. I am saying the evidence does not justify attributing the text to Allah when ordinary human processes already explain what we observe.

This makes it difficult for your partner to win merely by showing that revelation remains conceivable.

## 15. One-minute version

> The Qur'an's divine authorship is a claim, not a default. What we can observe is a thoroughly human chain: Muhammad publicly recites material in a particular language and culture; followers memorize and write it; after his death, authorities collect it because reciters are dying; later disagreement prompts an official recension and destruction of other written material. The text itself repeatedly addresses the speaker's immediate disputes, household and privileges, and it participates in the stories and concepts of its historical environment. All of that is expected if the Qur'an has a human history. A believer can say Allah worked through it, but possibility is not evidence. The Qur'an cannot independently certify itself, preservation would not prove authorship, and literary uniqueness does not logically identify God. So the reasonable conclusion is not that I know every human contributor; it is that human processes explain the evidence and Allah's authorship has not been demonstrated.

## 16. Two-minute closing statement

We began with a question of authorship, and that question must be answered by evidence rather than by repeating the attribution made inside the text.

The evidence available to us is human at every observable point. A man recites. People remember and write. Deaths create fear of loss. A collector searches memories and fragments. Disagreement leads a ruler to appoint a committee, impose a standard and destroy alternatives. Reports inside the tradition preserve traces of recited material outside the codex. The resulting text speaks repeatedly to the immediate conflicts, relationships and needs of the person delivering it, in the language and religious world of its period.

None of this mathematically disproves that Allah might have operated behind the scenes. But “might have” is not the proposition being defended. The proposition is that Allah did author these words. For that conclusion, we need evidence that distinguishes divine revelation from human composition, sincere inspiration, oral development and later canonization.

Self-testimony does not provide that distinction. Literary admiration does not provide it. Preservation, even if perfect, would not identify the original author. Appeals to hidden divine guidance can reconcile almost any historical outcome with faith, but an explanation compatible with every possible outcome predicts none of them.

The responsible conclusion is therefore limited but strong: the Qur'an we possess has an observable human history and humanly intelligible content. Divine authorship is a theological interpretation added to that history, not a conclusion established by it.

## 17. Rehearsal plan

### Round one: clarity

Deliver the one-minute version until you can say it conversationally without memorizing exact wording.

### Round two: hostile interruptions

Have a partner interrupt after every sentence with one of these:

- “Compilation is not authorship.”
- “Allah promised preservation.”
- “Muhammad was illiterate.”
- “Nobody has met the challenge.”
- “Earlier stories came from earlier revelation.”
- “The Qur'an criticizes Muhammad.”

Answer each in no more than twenty seconds, then return to the argument you were making.

### Round three: source discipline

For every historical claim, be able to say one of:

- “This is in the Qur'anic text itself.”
- “I am using this as a report accepted inside the Sunni tradition.”
- “This is a historical claim that I still need to verify.”

If you cannot classify a claim, do not use it yet.

### Round four: steelman

Practice stating your partner's argument in a form they accept before replying:

> “If I understand you, your claim is not that the committee was divine, but that Allah guaranteed a human committee would preserve an earlier verbatim revelation. Is that correct?”

This prevents talking past one another and makes the actual unsupported bridge visible.

## 18. Evidence-confidence map for this draft

### Use now as the backbone

- The distinction between a text's self-claim and independent evidence.
- The logical gap from inimitability or uniqueness to divine authorship.
- The distinction between preservation and authorship.
- The basic Abu Bakr/Zayd collection account recorded at Bukhari 4986.
- The basic Uthmanic standardization and destruction account recorded at Bukhari 4987.
- Direct Qur'anic passages concerning Muhammad's circumstances and privileges, provided they are quoted accurately and in context.

### Use with explicit qualification

- Umar's missing stoning recitation: internally researched as supported, but the doctrinal reply of abrogated recitation must be represented fairly.
- Aisha's suckling report: internally researched as supported, but its exact relationship to the final codex is the disputed point.
- Aisha's “hastens to your desire” remark: a valuable report, but the project has not yet completed its full evidence assessment.
- Mary/Aaron/Imran: the relevant wording is clear, but the interpretation is contested and has established Muslim replies.
- Environmental narrative parallels: useful cumulatively, but direction of dependence must not be assumed.

### Hold until separately verified

- strong claims that companion codices differed in sura totals or contained major substantive differences;
- the claim that Uthman destroyed original materials demonstrably dating to Muhammad;
- exact timelines for the addition of dots, vowels and hamza signs;
- specific Syriac re-pointings or wholesale alternative readings;
- al-Samiri and Haman chronology as decisive errors;
- claims of late Umayyad composition of the Qur'an;
- claims that any particular named intermediary supplied Muhammad with Qur'anic stories.

## 19. Final reminder

Your best line is not:

> “I have proved that Allah had nothing to do with the Qur'an.”

It is:

> “We agree on the human process. You are adding a divine cause. Show me the evidence that requires or independently identifies that cause.”

That keeps the burden where it belongs and prevents the discussion from becoming an endless attempt to disprove explanations invented only after each difficulty appears.
